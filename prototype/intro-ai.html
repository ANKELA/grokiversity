What AI is

AI 101 · Unit 1 · Concept 1 of 4 · agents, history, expected utility, search counts, Bayes, baselines

Objectives
  • Define an agent and distinguish rational action from imitating humans.
  • Compute expected utility and choose the action that maximises it.
  • Count nodes generated by breadth-first search, apply Bayes' rule, and compute a majority-class baseline.

The rational-agent view

Artificial intelligence, in the sense used by Russell & Norvig and by courses such as Stanford CS221 and Berkeley CS188, is the study of agents that receive percepts from an environment and take actions in order to do well according to a performance measure. The Turing test asks a different question — can a machine imitate a human? A thermostat that keeps a room at 20 °C is a better example of a rational agent than a chatbot that merely sounds human.

Describe a task by PEAS: Performance measure, Environment, Actuators, Sensors. Under uncertainty, a rational agent maximises expected utility:

$$\mathrm{EU}(a)=\sum_s P(s\mid a)\,U(s),\qquad a^\star=\arg\max_a \mathrm{EU}(a)$$

A short history, for orientation

Turing's 1950 paper, the 1956 Dartmouth workshop that named the field, symbolic systems and the first AI winter, 1980s expert systems and a second winter, statistical machine learning, the 2012 deep-learning wave, the 2017 transformer, and today's large language models. The tools changed. The questions — search, uncertainty, learning, decision-making — did not. Later units of this course take each of those questions in depth.

Two quantities you will need immediately

In a complete tree of branching factor \(b\), the number of nodes from the root (depth 0) down to depth \(d\) is \((b^{d+1}-1)/(b-1)\). If breadth-first search tests the goal when a node is generated, a worst-case goal at depth \(d\) requires generating every one of those nodes.

And Bayes' rule, which will get a full unit later:

$$P(H\mid E)=\frac{P(E\mid H)\,P(H)}{P(E)}$$

$$P(E)=P(E\mid H)P(H)+P(E\mid\neg H)P(\neg H)$$

Before you celebrate a classifier's accuracy, compute the majority-class baseline: \((\text{count of the most common class})/n\).

Linear models and mean squared error

AI 101 · Unit 1 · Concept 2 of 4 · ŷ = wx + b, MSE, ordinary least squares

Objectives
  • Write a linear predictor and compute its mean squared error on a finite data set.
  • Derive the ordinary-least-squares slope and intercept in one dimension.
  • Fit a no-intercept model, and compute the gradient of MSE with respect to \(w\) and \(b\).

A linear model is \(\hat y = w x + b\). Residuals are \(e_i = y_i - \hat y_i\). This course uses

$$\mathrm{MSE}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i-\hat y_i)^2$$

not the \(\tfrac{1}{2n}\) variant (that one only changes the gradient by a factor of 2; pick a convention and stick to it). The ordinary-least-squares minimiser is

$$\hat w=\frac{\sum(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2},\qquad \hat b=\bar y-\hat w\bar x.$$

The gradient, which Concept 3 will use, is

$$\frac{\partial\mathrm{MSE}}{\partial w}=-\frac{2}{n}\sum_i x_i e_i,\qquad \frac{\partial\mathrm{MSE}}{\partial b}=-\frac{2}{n}\sum_i e_i.$$

Gradient descent

AI 101 · Unit 1 · Concept 3 of 4 · the update, learning rates, overshoot

Objectives
  • Write a gradient-descent update and carry out steps by hand.
  • Take one MSE step for a linear model from a given \((w,b)\).
  • Find the critical learning rate on a one-dimensional quadratic, and recognise oscillation versus divergence.

Gradient descent minimises a differentiable loss \(J(\theta)\) by walking downhill:

$$\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\nabla J(\theta)$$

The learning rate \(\eta>0\) is a step size. For the quadratic \(J(w)=(w-3)^2\), we have \(J'(w)=2(w-3)\) and the recurrence \(w\leftarrow(1-2\eta)w+6\eta\). This converges to 3 if and only if \(0<\eta<1\). At \(\eta=1\) it oscillates; for \(\eta>1\) it diverges. A step can also increase \(J\) when \(\eta\) is large relative to the curvature — always recompute the loss; don't assume it fell.

A first neuron, and attention

AI 101 · Unit 1 · Concept 4 of 4 · sigmoid units, softmax, scaled dot-product attention

Objectives
  • Compute the pre-activation and sigmoid output of a single neuron.
  • Compute a softmax and the corresponding weighted sum of values.
  • Compute scaled dot-product attention for a query and a small set of keys and values.

A neuron is a linear unit plus a nonlinearity: \(z=w\cdot x+b\), then \(a=\sigma(z)\) with \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\). Stacking many of these, and differentiating with the chain rule, is Unit 7.

Attention is also a weighted sum, but the weights depend on the input. Given scores \(s_i\),

$$\alpha_i=\frac{e^{s_i}}{\sum_j e^{s_j}},\qquad \mathrm{output}=\sum_i \alpha_i v_i.$$

The vector \(\alpha\) is a probability distribution, so the output is a convex combination of the values. Scaled dot-product attention (Vaswani et al., 2017) uses \(s_i=(q\cdot k_i)/\sqrt{d}\). Dividing by \(\sqrt{d}\) keeps the scores from growing with dimension, which would make softmax one-hot. Pretraining, fine-tuning and RLHF are Unit 9; this is the arithmetic kernel.
